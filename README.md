## Introduction

Manipulation tasks such as grasping and lifting involve complex, non-smooth dynamics due to contact transitions and often allow for multiple valid approaches to the object. Moreover, partial observability makes it difficult for an agent to infer the underlying physical state from its observations, increasing the complexity of policy learning. While model-free reinforcement learning algorithms such as Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) [3] and Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) [4] have demonstrated their ability to learn such tasks without explicitly modeling the system dynamics, they may require extensive interaction data to converge towards effective policies. Autoregressive world models offer a complementary approach to learning system dynamics and have demonstrated promising results in robotic control, particularly in the area of interaction efficiency. One such approach is the Robotic World Model (RWM) [1] and its uncertainty-aware extension, RWM-U [2], which use autoregressive recurrent models to predict future robot observations. Building on this approach, we investigate its applicability to manipulation in simulation using a Franka cube-lifting task.

## Related Work

RWM [1] uses multi-step autoregressive training of a recurrent model to predict future robot observations and privileged physical quantities, such as contacts. It demonstrates accurate long-horizon predictions across locomotion and manipulation tasks, but its experiments focus on locomotion. Its extension, RWM-U [2], introduces ensemble-based epistemic uncertainty estimation and uncertainty-penalized offline policy optimization. While RWM-U also evaluates policy learning on a Franka reaching task, neither work investigates world-model-based approach planning for approach trajectories and grasping. We therefore aim to investigate whether incorporating geometric information improves the prediction of contact-rich dynamics and whether uncertainty-aware world models can help select robust approach motions under perception errors.

## Technical Outline

Similar to the discussed approaches, our architecture operates directly on explicit physical variables as observations. The core of the architecture is an ensemble consisting of $K$ independent neural networks. At each timestep $t$, the input to the ensemble is the concatenated physical state vector $s_t$, which includes the robot's joint kinematics, the object's $SE(3)$ pose, and privileged binary contact arrays, alongside the commanded action $a_t$. Each network $k$ predicts the state residual $\Delta s_t^{(k)}$, outputting the next state as $s_{t+1}^{(k)} = s_t + \Delta s_t^{(k)}$. By training against these explicit targets, we expect to force the world model to learn to distinguish potentially unsafe approaches to the object from reliable ones.

The predictions of the $K$ ensemble networks enable the estimation of epistemic uncertainty, denoted by $u_t$. In the original RWM-U framework, this uncertainty estimate is used to regularize policy optimization, by augmenting the reward with a penalty term, $R(\mu_{t+1}) - \lambda u_t$. This discourages the policy from exploiting regions of the prediction space where the world model is less reliable and prone to error. We hypothesize that, in manipulation tasks, epistemic uncertainty may also provide a useful signal about approach and grasp quality, reflecting the model’s confidence in its predictions during contact and lifting.

Thus, the setup phase of this project largely follows the procedure outlined in the original paper.

- Integrate the core RWM-U framework into the `Isaac-Franka-Lift` environment.
- Collect interaction data using an expert and pretrain the world model ensemble.
- Train a model-free policy using the pretrained ensemble as a learned simulator.

Building on the findings of the original paper, we will investigate two approaches to data collection: (1) a state-machine-based expert and (2) trajectories generated by a model-free RL algorithm. Since the training data distribution can significantly influence the accuracy and generalization of the learned world model, we will examine, subject to time constraints, how different data collection strategies affect subsequent policy optimization. We expect the results to be sensitive to hyperparameter tuning and will investigate whether the model's uncertainty estimates reflect differences in gripper approach quality and, if so, how well. We will also evaluate the robustness of the learned policies under domain randomization, including variations in object pose, mass and dimensions.

As a potential extension, we plan to investigate how the learned world model responds to obstacles when their locations are provided as input, and evaluate the resulting effects on the downstream optimization of the policy.
